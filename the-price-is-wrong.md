# The Price Is Wrong

## 0.  Introduction

**When considering new renewables investment (as most renewables are independent projects), overall power price or costs shouldn't the key metrics, expected profit should be.** 

### I. 

> "...to look predominantly at price when thinking about what makes for a successful (or failed) transition is to look in the wrong place."
>
> "The better, more meaningful yardstick is profit... most governments worldwide have effectively outsourced responsibility for developing, owning and operating solar and wind farms to profit-oriented private sector actors, and yet the profits that such actors expect to be able to earn from investments in these activities generally underwhelm."
>
> "...there are all sorts of reasons why lower costs of generation do not automatically equate to higher profits."

### II. 

> "Already, in other words, we are far off the pace of investment in clean power that is required if we are going to have any reasonable prospect of making good on our historic failures."

### III.

> "As the Economist put it in 2022, the world's current decarbonisation strategy can effective by captured in two words: 'Electrify everything'."

> "But our focus will be on the large, commercially oriented, 'utility-scale' renewables developments that, ultimately, will make or break most countries' attempts to decarbonise electricity."
>
> "When a commercial entity makes a decision about a potential investment in, say, new wind power capacity, the choice is almost always between wind farm and no wind farm, not wind farm and gas-fired plant, and thus the relevant profitability benchmark is the rate of return that the investor considers acceptable rather than the rate of return on dirty generation assets."

### IV. 

> "Use values - in this case, electricity - were produced only because they had to be; they were not the company's raison d'être... actually producing electrical power was 'a frankly incidental component' of the business of producing profit; 'if [the company] could have done the world thing synthetically they probably world have'... still less did they care if that the real thing was environmentally friendly or not."

### V. 

> "If a renewable energy developer needs capital from a financial institution of some kind in order to be able to proceed with a new power plant, then it is, ultimately, finance capital that decides whether the development will go ahead or not."

### VI. 

> "Renewable energy generation is a business perennially plagued by investor doubts about expected profits, and this is the reason why economic support from the public sector, as we will see, remains integral to sustaining the appetite for investment in new solar and wind capacity more or less everywhere in the world."

## 2. The Business of Power

**The power industry has developed into a neoliberal market, with unbundling (of national electricity sectors), de-monopolisation, privatisation, and marketisation.**

**This means that renewables generators are typically non-vertically integrated, private companies**

### I. 

> "But the basic story of electricity is the basic story of 'neoliberalisation' - the amalgam of processes whereby neoliberalism has effectively been engineered on the ground."

### II. Unbundling

> "The tendency for generation and other core industry activities increasingly to be separated from one another, and for the vertically integrated entities... to be dis-integrated"
>
> "Wind- and solar-based electricity generation is in large measure a discrete, standalone business".

### III. De-monopolisation

> "Vertical unbundling was considered necessary - that is, to facilitate competition."
    >
    > "Companies could not be expected to compete effectively in one business area if lumbered with another, especially if the latter activity was not itself amenable to competition"
    >
    > "Conflicts of interest could arise if governments were trying to engender competition without having first unbundled."

### IV. Privatisation

> "Insofar as the private sector is ruled by the profit imperative, profitability - its determinants, availability and scale - utterly dominates renewable energy's conditions of both possibility and performance."

### V. Marketisation

> "The turning over of sectoral transactions of all sorts to theoretically unfettered exchange between market actors."

### VI. 

> "The key learning is that the generation of electricity is increasingly a separate business"
>
> "As well as using being separate from the rest of the supply chain, electricity generation is usually competitive - much more than any of transmission, distribution or retail. The entities that compete in electricity generation, and especially renewables generation, are also typically private corporations, existing to generate profit."
>
> "It is more and more the case that what types of generation capacity installed, how much electricity is then produced, and how much the customers of generating companies pay for that electricity are all determined by market mechanisms of various kinds, rather than by, say, direct, top-down government prescription."

### Other.

> "Most generations of solar and wind power are not vertically integrated into downstream parts of the electricity supply chain: they are not active in transmission, distribution or retail." (4.IV)

## 4. The Price Is Right?

**In recent years, the (development) cost of renewables has fallen dramatically, via government supports and manufacturing advances.**

| Category                | Mechanism                          | Example                              |
|:------------------------|:-----------------------------------|:-------------------------------------|
| **Investment Support**  | Construction subsidisation         | Tax credit (US)                      |
| **Financial Support**   | Financing subsidisation            | CERF (China), CRFO (Japan)           |
| **Demand Stimulus**     | Supplier procurement obligations   | RO Scheme (UK), RPO (India)          |

### I & II. Generation Source Transition

> "The critical factor to consider is the relative price paid for power: if electricity can be generated more cheaply from renewable sources than from fossil fuels, then the former will surge ahead; but if fossil-fuel-based power is cheaper to produce, then it, instead, will remain dominant."

> "The 'economic style of reasoning' took over... environmental problems increasingly came to be seen as economic problems. Mores specifically still, they came to be seen as price problems."
>
> "Historians have found that for a 'full' energy transition from one energy form to another to take place, the price of the winning technology must be lower than another... only one factor - a lower price - is necessary for the transition to occur."

### III. Government intervention methods

>
> Indirect revenue support via demand stimulation/obligations
>
> Incremental revenue stream specifically for renewably generated energy
>
> Price control

## 5. The Price Is Wrong

**Focusing on lower construction costs/ LCOE alone fail to show whether renewables investment projects will succeed or fail...**  

### I. 

> "'Both effective supply and demand are regulated, through different channels, by expected profitability'. Should they not foresee profitability... capitalists do not invest"
>
> "The investment decisions that drove the early nineteenth-century energy transition ultimately hinged not on price, but on profit"

### II. Conditions on whether lowest-cost options maximise profit

> Whether the industry is vertically integrated, "relative costs tend only to win out if it is."
>
> The relationship between costs and revenues, and the ability to retain profit.
>
> Nature of the product, "do the two energy sources generate the exact same product with, accordingly, the exact same revenue?"

### III. Vertical integration

> "All else being equal... [a vertically integrated company] will generate electricity renewably if the costs of doing so are lower than the costs of conventional generation... The LCOE rules."
>
> "These [companies] typically have delivery obligations, and thus need to procure supply, and the choice they face is between electricity from different sources - renewable or not."

> "As for the independent producer... just because it can generate power at a certain cost, there is no certainty that is has the necessary incentive to do so."
>
> "The independent producer meanwhile does not have to develop the project, and a financial institution does not have to fund it."

### IV. Relationship between costs and revenues

> "Generators would not stand to gain much, if at all, because [neoliberal markets are] where the coercive law of price competition operates with the fullest force... meaning that cost reductions are perforce passed on... the particular industry constituency that the world is relying on to actually make the switch to renewables - that is, generating companies... - is seemingly not the industry constituency that would stand most to gain economically from that switch" (5.IV)

### V. Nature of the product/ The issues with LCOE

**Due to the non-dispatchable nature of renewables, they typically are forced to generate while power prices are lowest, especially for sites in regions with the highest concentration of renewables (Scotland for wind, Spain and Germany for solar)** 

> "The availability and cost of land vary enormously... from place to place. Where available land is abundant, it tends to be cheap."
> 
> "Developers of wind and solar power have a strong incentive to undertake development where land is cheapest."
>
> "The fact that renewables facilities are disproportionately located in relatively remote locations with relatively cheap land is on important reason why their LCOE is as low as it is."

> "One of the problems with the basic LCOE comparisons that are often made, however, is that they are rendered in temporal as well as spatial abstraction."
>
> "There exists a vast legacy infrastructure of conventional plants, much of the lifetime costs of which have already been incurred, 'sunk' costs... These old, particularly or completely amortised plants, which may very well have a high LCOE, are, in practice, at least if fuel costs are low, produce extremely cheap power."
>
> "One plant will almost inevitably produce and sell power at times of higher average prices than the other... Temporal variation in electricity pricing that is of such a magnitude as to raise the distinct possibility that relative LCOEs are essentially meaningless as a guide to the likely profitability of different types of generating plant."

> "When companies set about developing wind and solar farms, and banks set about making decisions whether to finance those developments, LCOE numbers are, in reality, among the very furthest things from their minds."

### VI. Projected revenue 

> "In a depressed market environment, wind's favourable LCOE did not make the slightest bit of difference [to investment decisions, due to low power prices]."

## 6. The Wild West

**In neoliberal power markets, spot markets are highly common and demonstrate high levels of price volatility. This volatility is a key concern for investors, fearful of non-guaranteed revenues to cover loan payback. Government support can thus enable either cost reduction or, (increasingly) more importantly, price stability.**

> "In 2018, something remarkable happened to the UK's onshore wind industry... it was the lowest level of annual installation of new onshore wind generating capacity that the country had seen since 2011."
>
> The UK's RO scheme closed in 2017, "Denied, thus, any kind of 'market stabilisation mechanism', onshore wind... was abruptly subject to 'the volatility of the market'." (6.V)

> "We have focused in this chapter on barriers to investment in renewables associated with uncertainty around expected revenues and profits, and rooted specifically in market and price volatility." (6.VI)
>
> "In countries with liberalised electricity sectors featuring wholesale spot markets, a fundamental constraint on investment in renewables capacity is a lack of visibility and predictability with regard to expected profitability" via "electricity prices or the revenues of generators that sell into spot markets." (7.I)

### I. UK Spot Market characteristics

> Single-clearing price mechanism: "Generators prepare bids for particular settlement periods of half-hourly generation, specifying the quantity of generation... and the price at which they are willing to sell it." + "All electricity traded for a particular settlement period is priced equally, and this uniform price represents the bid offered by the highest-bid generator amount those dispatched."
>
> Merit order dispatch
>
> "Bidding into the merit order is based on generators' marginal costs"

### II. Spot market volatility 

> "The signature characteristic of electricity spot markets based on merit order dispatch is price volatility, existing at all temporal scales."
>
> "Electricity price volatility is the result sometimes primarily of fuel price volatility, sometimes primarily of changes in the identity of the provider of the last in-merit unit of power, and sometimes of both."

### III. Volatility consequences

> "In the absence of overt mechanisms of price stabilisation, spot market price volatility has a propounding chilling effect on renewables investments."
>
> "It is in fact not developers themselves who are typically adverse to volatility risk... the decision aversion is usually that of another actor - namely the banker."
>
> "The obstacle is the effect of price volatility on the perceived infeasibility of projects under development... 'bankability'."

### IV. Methods of volatility hedging

> "Where conventional plants are developed today in countries which the bulk of electricity is sold through spot markets, the volatility of such markets does not imperil the financing of such plants to anything like the degree that is problematics renewables financing.... Bank's concern with price volatility... is on the downside."
>
> "When spot market electricity prices fall, so also... do gas prices. In fact [power prices] fall precisely because [gas prices] fall."
>
> "The use of merit order dispatch and the status of gas as the predominant source of the last unit of dispatched power combine in such a way as to afford a certain symmetry of revenue and cost dynamics for gas-fired generating plants... there is an inherent hedge... Renewables plants enjoy no such hedge."

> "Three main categories of [price stability-oriented] mechanisms exist:" 
> - Power Purchase Agreements
> - Hedging via futures and swaps
> - Government intervention

### V. Government intervention

> "Some [government] instruments do not contribute to hedging price volatility risk" like incremental revenue programmes or investment/financial support.
>
> "Instruments of government support based on price controls can explicitly provide stability" like FiT and CfD

> When investment costs were high, government support was to subsidise but also to stabilise, "now that renewables are not relatively dear, government support... is designed primarily to stabilise."
>
> "Should such a blanket [of support] be summarily removed, there would be a distinctively chilling impact on investment appetite."

### VI. Global market direction

> "The world is moving towards electricity spot markets, price volatility and correspondingly poor visibility around the expected profitability of renewables generation."
>
> "The result, absent meaningful extrinsic mechanisms of stabilisation, can be dramatic chilling of investment."
